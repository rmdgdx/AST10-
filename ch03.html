§From genetic information to the diversity of life

Week 3 connects four questions: How is biological information stored and expressed? How do populations change? How do we name and reconstruct relationships among organisms? What do microorganisms reveal about the limits of life?

A microbe in a hot spring is more than an example of tolerance. Its inherited sequences influence proteins and membranes; its population has an evolutionary history; its scientific name identifies a taxon; and its physiology helps us evaluate a possible planetary habitat. This lecture follows that chain of reasoning without treating an Earth analogue as proof of extraterrestrial life.

  • Trace DNA replication, transcription, and translation using a short sequence.
  • Predict simple inheritance outcomes and distinguish mutation, selection, drift, and gene flow.
  • Explain reproductive isolation and the limits of species concepts for microorganisms.
  • Apply binomial nomenclature, use a dichotomous key, and interpret a cladogram.
  • Compare the domains of cellular life and microbial modes of growth and nutrition.
  • Evaluate extremophile adaptations and construct the required phylogeny and extremophile portfolio.

1DNA and gene expression

DNA is a sequence, not a finished trait

A DNA nucleotide contains deoxyribose, phosphate, and one of four bases: adenine (A), thymine (T), cytosine (C), or guanine (G). Complementary pairing joins A with T and C with G. The two strands run in opposite directions, designated 5′ and 3′. A gene is a DNA sequence whose expression produces a functional RNA or a polypeptide; not every gene encodes a protein. A genome is the complete genetic complement, while a chromosome is a long DNA molecule with associated proteins.

Before cell division, semiconservative replication produces daughter DNA molecules containing one parental strand and one new strand. DNA polymerases extend new DNA in the 5′→3′ direction using a template. Proofreading and repair reduce errors but do not eliminate them. Replication copies the genome; gene expression uses selected parts of it.

ProcessInput → productEssential machineryBiological role
ReplicationDNA → DNADNA polymerase and other replication proteinsTransmit genetic information to daughter cells.
TranscriptionDNA template → RNARNA polymeraseProduce mRNA, rRNA, tRNA, and other RNAs.
TranslationmRNA → polypeptideRibosome, tRNAs, amino acidsDecode mRNA into an amino-acid sequence.

Follow one gene from sequence to product

RNA polymerase reads the DNA template 3′→5′ while synthesizing RNA 5′→3′. RNA contains uracil (U) instead of thymine. The RNA sequence matches the coding strand except that U replaces T. During translation, the ribosome reads the mRNA in three-base codons; tRNA anticodons pair with codons and deliver amino acids. An initiation context establishes the reading frame. AUG commonly starts translation and encodes methionine; UAA, UAG, and UGA normally signal termination.

Worked sequence · a simplified coding region

DNA coding strand: 5′–ATG GAA TTT TGA–3′
DNA template strand: 3′–TAC CTT AAA ACT–5′
mRNA: 5′–AUG GAA UUU UGA–3′
Polypeptide: methionine–glutamate–phenylalanine, followed by termination.

The stop codon does not add a “stop amino acid.” If the coding strand is given, replace T with U; if the template is given, use complementary pairing and check direction.

There are 64 codons in the standard code: 61 specify the 20 standard amino acids and three are stop signals. Several codons can specify the same amino acid, so the code is degenerate, but a codon normally has one meaning in a given code. Its near-universality supports common ancestry of known cellular life; exceptions occur, including in some organelles. This evidence does not identify the first organism or prove that all possible life must use our code.

Regulation links a shared genome to different functions

Most nucleated cells in an individual share broadly the same genome but express different sets of genes. Regulatory proteins, promoter accessibility, RNA processing and stability, and translation influence how much product is made. In eukaryotes, protein-coding transcripts commonly undergo capping, splicing, and polyadenylation before translation in the cytoplasm. Bacteria lack a nucleus, allowing transcription and translation to be coupled. Environmental signals can change expression without changing DNA sequence: a heat-shock response is not automatically an inherited mutation.

Check your reasoning: More mRNA, same DNA?
If a nutrient causes more transcript to accumulate while the DNA sequence stays unchanged, regulation of transcription or RNA stability is a better initial explanation than mutation. Protein abundance may still differ because translation and degradation also matter.

2Inheritance: alleles, meiosis, and probability

An allele is an alternative sequence at a genetic locus. A diploid individual ordinarily carries two alleles at an autosomal locus, one from each parent. Genotype describes that genetic constitution; phenotype describes an observable characteristic resulting from genotype, environment, and their interaction. Homozygotes carry the same allele twice; heterozygotes carry different alleles. A dominant allele is expressed in the heterozygote under the specified inheritance model; “dominant” does not mean more common, healthier, or evolutionarily superior.

Mendel's law of segregation reflects the separation of homologous chromosomes during meiosis: an Aa parent contributes A to approximately half its gametes and a to the other half. Fertilization combines gametes probabilistically. Independent assortment applies to unlinked loci, such as genes on different chromosomes; nearby genes on the same chromosome can be inherited together. Crossing over produces recombination, but linked genes need not yield the simple ratios predicted for independent assortment.

Worked monohybrid cross: Aa × Aa

Equal gamete probabilities; complete dominance of A
Parent 1 gamete ↓ / Parent 2 →A (½)a (½)
A (½)AA (¼)Aa (¼)
a (½)Aa (¼)aa (¼)

The expected genotype ratio is 1 AA : 2 Aa : 1 aa. With complete dominance, the phenotype ratio is 3 dominant : 1 recessive. These are probabilities across many offspring, not a promise that every four offspring contain one of each predicted category. A testcross pairs an individual of unknown dominant genotype with aa: a large sample approaching 1:1 supports Aa, whereas all dominant offspring are consistent with AA but cannot establish it with certainty in a small sample.

PatternInterpretationExample of reasoning
Incomplete dominanceThe heterozygote is intermediate.A red × white cross can yield pink offspring; alleles have not permanently blended.
CodominanceBoth allele-associated products are expressed.AB blood type expresses A and B antigens.
Polygenic inheritanceSeveral loci contribute to a trait.Continuous variation need not fit a single 3:1 ratio.

These diploid crosses cannot simply be imposed on a haploid bacterial population. Bacteria commonly reproduce by binary fission and inherit DNA vertically from a parent cell, while horizontal transfer can introduce additional genetic material. Always identify ploidy, reproductive mode, and assumptions before choosing a genetic model.

3Mutation and the origins of variation

A mutation is a change in nucleotide sequence. It can arise from replication errors, chemical changes to bases, mobile genetic elements, or exposure to mutagens. Recombination reshuffles existing variants, whereas mutation can generate new alleles. In sexually reproducing multicellular organisms, a germline mutation can enter subsequent generations; a somatic mutation usually affects a cell lineage within the individual. In a reproducing microbe, a mutation can be transmitted to daughter cells.

Sequence changePossible consequenceWorked example / limitation
Synonymous substitutionNo amino-acid changemRNA GAA → GAG still encodes glutamate; effects on expression are nevertheless possible.
Missense substitutionOne amino acid changesGAA → GUA changes glutamate to valine; impact depends on the protein site.
Nonsense substitutionA sense codon becomes a stop codonGAA → UAA can terminate translation early.
Insertion/deletionA coding-region change not divisible by three shifts the frameA one-base insertion changes downstream grouping; a three-base insertion ordinarily preserves the frame.
Regulatory mutationAmount, timing, or location of expression changesA promoter alteration can affect RNA production without changing the protein's coding sequence.
Duplication or rearrangementCopy number or genomic context changesA duplicated gene can retain, lose, or diverge in function over evolutionary time.

Mutation effects are context-dependent. A sequence variant may be harmful in one environment, nearly neutral in another, and beneficial in a third. Neither every mutation nor every amino-acid substitution produces a visible phenotype. A frameshift early in a coding region is often disruptive, but “always lethal” would be an unjustified claim.

Mutation is not a response designed to meet a need

Mutations are not directed toward the particular adaptive outcome an organism needs. Mutation rates and locations can vary, and stress can affect mutation processes, but this is different from bacteria deliberately producing the correct resistance mutation. Antibiotic exposure can favor cells already carrying resistance; mutation and gene transfer can also supply variants during population growth. Selection changes which variants leave descendants.

Three routes of horizontal gene transferTransformation: uptake of environmental DNA. Transduction: transfer mediated by a virus infecting bacteria. Conjugation: DNA transfer through direct cell-to-cell contact, often involving plasmids. These processes can spread alleles between lineages; they do not themselves increase the number of cells.
Check your reasoning: Is a three-base deletion a frameshift?
Ordinarily no, if it lies within a coding region and removes exactly three bases. It preserves downstream triplet grouping, although loss or alteration of an amino acid can still damage protein function.

4Evolution and natural selection

Evolution includes changes in the inherited composition of populations across generations. Individuals can develop, acclimatize, or change gene expression during life; those changes alone are not population evolution. Natural selection requires phenotypic variation, a heritable component of that variation, and differences in reproductive success associated with it. Fitness means relative contribution of descendants in a particular environment, not physical strength or progress toward perfection.

MechanismHow frequencies changeDiagnostic scenario
Natural selectionHeritable variants differ in reproductive contribution.A resistant lineage leaves more descendants under a treatment.
Genetic driftChance sampling changes allele frequencies; effects are stronger in small populations.A few colonists carry an unrepresentative set of alleles: founder effect.
Gene flowMigration followed by reproduction moves alleles among populations.Immigrants contribute alleles to the receiving population.
MutationSequence changes generate new alleles.A previously absent nucleotide variant appears.

Worked selection example

Imagine a haploid culture with 900 susceptible and 100 resistant cells. Resistant frequency is initially 100/1,000 = 0.10. In a simplified treatment model, 10% of susceptible cells and 80% of resistant cells survive: 90 susceptible and 80 resistant remain. Resistant frequency among survivors is now 80/170 ≈ 0.471. The resistant population did not need to grow for its relative frequency to rise. If survivors reproduce and the resistance is inherited, their descendants can carry the changed composition forward. Real cultures also involve growth rates, costs, density effects, and gene transfer.

A sudden population reduction can create a bottleneck; if survival is random with respect to the allele, resulting changes reflect drift. A bottleneck and selection can occur together, so reduced population size alone does not identify the mechanism. Selection can favor one end of a trait distribution, intermediate values, or both extremes, depending on the relationship between trait and fitness.

A quantitative extension: Hardy–Weinberg as a reference model

For a diploid, two-allele locus, let p be the frequency of A and q that of a. Under the model's assumptions—random mating, a very large population, and no selection, migration, or mutation—expected genotype frequencies are:

p + q = 1    and    p² (AA) + 2pq (Aa) + q² (aa) = 1

If p = 0.6 and q = 0.4, the expected frequencies are 0.36 AA, 0.48 Aa, and 0.16 aa. In 100 individuals, these correspond to expected counts of 36, 48, and 16. A discrepancy prompts investigation, but it does not uniquely establish natural selection. Nonrandom mating, population structure, sampling variation, and genotyping error can affect observed genotype proportions; not every departure demonstrates a change in allele frequencies over time.

Further reading: OpenStax · Population Evolution.

5Speciation and reproductive isolation

Speciation is the formation of distinct evolutionary lineages. For sexually reproducing organisms, the biological species concept emphasizes actual or potential interbreeding in nature and reproductive isolation from other groups. It is useful but not universal: fossils cannot be tested by mating, asexual organisms do not fit its central criterion, and some distinct lineages hybridize.

Allopatric speciation begins with geographic separation that reduces gene flow. Mutation, drift, and selection can then promote divergence. Separation is an opportunity for divergence, not automatic proof of a new species. Sympatric speciation occurs without a geographic barrier; changes in host use, mating, or chromosome number can reduce gene exchange. Polyploidy is particularly relevant in plants, linking this week to botany.

BarrierWhen it actsExample
Habitat or temporal isolationBefore fertilizationPopulations occupy different mating habitats or reproduce in different seasons.
Behavioral isolationBefore fertilizationDifferent courtship signals reduce mating.
Mechanical or gametic isolationBefore fertilizationReproductive structures or gamete recognition are incompatible.
Hybrid inviabilityAfter fertilizationHybrid offspring fail to develop normally or survive.
Hybrid sterility or breakdownAfter fertilizationHybrids are sterile, or later-generation offspring have reduced fitness.

Microbial classification therefore combines other evidence: genome similarity, phylogenetic placement, physiology, and ecology. A different growth temperature by itself does not establish a new microbial species. Conversely, two microbes with similar shapes may belong to deeply divergent lineages. Species boundaries are scientific hypotheses evaluated with multiple kinds of evidence, not just labels assigned by appearance.

Evidence auditTwo island populations breed at different times. This supports a prezygotic temporal barrier. To claim speciation, also assess gene exchange and lineage independence; geographic separation or a single observation cannot settle every case.

Further reading: OpenStax · Formation of New Species.

6Taxonomy, nomenclature, and identification

Taxonomy concerns describing and classifying organisms; nomenclature supplies naming rules; identification assigns an unknown to a recognized group. Systematics investigates diversity and evolutionary relationships. These tasks interact, but assigning a specimen a name is not the same as discovering its ancestry.

A familiar hierarchy is domain → kingdom → phylum → class → order → family → genus → species. A taxon is a named group at any rank. The hierarchy organizes nested membership, but equal ranks in different groups do not guarantee equal ages or equal genetic distances. Modern classifications are revised when new evidence changes relationships.

Write scientific names precisely

A species binomial consists of a capitalized genus and a lowercase specific epithet, both italicized: Escherichia coli. After the first unambiguous mention, E. coli is acceptable. The epithet “coli” alone is not the complete species name. When handwriting, underline each word separately. The name Halobacterium salinarum contains the genus Halobacterium and epithet salinarum; despite its name, the organism belongs to Archaea. Names are not substitutes for classification evidence.

Use a dichotomous key as an explicit decision process

A dichotomous key offers two contrasting statements at each step. Choose the statement supported by the observation, follow its destination, and repeat until a group is identified. A key is only valid for the organisms and characters it was designed to distinguish. An unknown that fits neither branch requires more evidence or another key, not a forced answer.

Teaching key for four specified groups; not a universal microbial key

1bNo membrane-bound nucleus; prokaryotic organization is confirmed.Go to 3.

For a nucleated, chloroplast-bearing sample, the path is 1a → 2a. “No nucleus visible” in a poor image is not equivalent to “no nucleus exists.” Some bacteria lack cell walls, so absence of peptidoglycan alone cannot classify every real specimen as an archaeon. Record the observations, route, and limitations together.

7Phylogeny and cladogram construction

A phylogeny is a hypothesis of evolutionary relationships. Tips represent sampled taxa; internal nodes represent inferred common ancestors; branches represent lineages. A rooted tree identifies a direction from an ancestral lineage. A clade contains an ancestor and all its descendants. Sister taxa share an immediate common ancestor in the sampled tree. Rotating branches around a node changes the drawing, not the relationship.

Which similarities count?

Homologous characters share an evolutionary origin. A shared derived character, or synapomorphy, supports a clade relative to an ancestral condition. A shared ancestral character may identify a broader group without resolving relationships within it. Convergence produces similar features independently; reversals can also obscure history. Homology is therefore a hypothesis tested with anatomical, developmental, or molecular evidence, not simply another word for resemblance.

An outgroup, chosen from outside the focal group, helps root the tree and infer character polarity. It is not an unchanged ancestor. Living organisms at all tips have their own evolutionary histories: a lineage that branches earlier in a diagram is not “less evolved.” A basal position alone cannot prove where life originated or the temperature preferred by the last universal common ancestor.

A reproducible classroom workflow

  1. Define the taxa and select comparable, plausibly homologous characters.
  2. Code states consistently in a matrix; distinguish missing data from absence.
  3. Choose an appropriate outgroup and propose alternative branching orders.
  4. Map state changes onto each tree. In simple parsimony, prefer the hypothesis requiring fewer changes under the chosen coding and weights.
  5. Report conflicting characters and uncertainty rather than hiding them.

For the exercise matrix, character 1 occurs in A–D, character 2 in B–D, and character 3 in C–D. Those nested distributions support the branching shown above. Character 4, shared only by A and D, conflicts with that pattern: on this tree it can be explained by independent gains or a gain followed by losses. Parsimony counts changes; it does not prove that evolution always took the shortest route.

Molecular trees compare aligned homologous sequences using explicit analytical models. Rapid change, repeated substitutions, unequal rates, limited sampling, or poor models can mislead reconstruction. Horizontal gene transfer can also make one gene's history differ from the organismal history. Multiple genes and support assessments improve evaluation, but uncertainty remains part of the result.

8Domains of life and microorganisms

“Microorganism” describes scale and a field of study, not one taxonomic lineage. It includes bacteria, archaea, and many eukaryotes, such as yeasts, microscopic algae, and diverse unicellular protists. Viruses are also studied in microbiology, but are acellular and depend on host cells for replication; they are not a fourth domain of cellular life.

FeatureBacteriaArchaeaEukarya
NucleusAbsentAbsentPresent in typical nucleated cells
MembranesTypically ester-linked fatty-acid lipidsCharacteristically ether-linked isoprenoid lipidsTypically ester-linked fatty-acid lipids
Cell wallUsually contains peptidoglycan; exceptions existNo peptidoglycan; walls varyVaries: cellulose in plants, chitin in fungi, none in animals

The three-domain framework distinguishes Bacteria, Archaea, and Eukarya using major cellular and molecular differences, historically including small-subunit ribosomal RNA evidence. It should not be confused with a final answer about the deepest branching topology. Phylogenomic research on Asgard archaea supports an origin of eukaryotes within the archaeal radiation. Naming three cellular groups and evaluating a two-primary-lineage evolutionary model address related but different questions.

Energy source, electron donor, and carbon source are separate axes

AxisAlternativesWhat to ask
EnergyPhototrophy / chemotrophyIs energy captured from light or chemical reactions?
Electron donorLithotrophy / organotrophyAre electrons obtained from inorganic or organic compounds?
CarbonAutotrophy / heterotrophyIs biomass carbon built mainly from CO₂ or preformed organic compounds?

A chemolithoautotroph can use chemical energy, an inorganic electron donor such as hydrogen, and CO₂ as its carbon source. This separates “no sunlight” from “no energy.” Sustained metabolism still requires suitable reaction partners, nutrients, water, and a usable energy yield. An electron donor alone does not establish habitability.

Microbes drive decomposition and nutrient cycling. Some fix atmospheric nitrogen; some oxidize sulfur compounds; others use nitrate or sulfate in anaerobic respiration. Methanogenesis is associated with particular archaea, whereas cyanobacteria perform oxygen-producing photosynthesis. Not all archaea make methane or inhabit extremes, and not all bacteria cause disease. In fermentation, cells regenerate electron carriers using internal organic intermediates rather than an external respiratory electron acceptor.

Growth, survival, and detection

During binary fission, a cell replicates its genetic material and divides. Under ideal exponential growth, N = N₀ × 2ⁿ, where n is the number of generations: 100 cells undergoing three doublings yield 800. A closed culture typically passes through lag, exponential, stationary, and decline phases; unlimited doubling is not a realistic long-term prediction. Some bacteria form dormant endospores for survival, not reproduction: one cell forming one spore does not create two cells.

A culture can miss organisms that do not grow under the chosen conditions. Sequence-based detection can reveal genetic material, but DNA detection alone does not demonstrate current metabolism or growth. Observation, chemistry, cultivation, molecular data, and contamination controls answer different questions and are strongest when combined.

Further reading: Prokaryotic Cells; Archaea; Microbial Growth; Asgard phylogenomics research.

9Extremophiles and planetary habitability

An extremophile grows optimally under conditions considered extreme in a conventional biological or human frame of reference. An extremotolerant organism tolerates a stress but need not prefer it. A polyextremophile is adapted to several extremes. These categories depend on measured growth responses, not on dramatic labels or the survival of one exposure.

Group / stressPhysiological challengePossible adaptationInterpretive caution
Thermophiles / high temperatureProtein unfolding and membrane instabilityProtein stabilization, chaperones, membrane adaptationsGrowth optimum differs from maximum survival temperature; hyperthermophiles have optima around 80°C or above.
Psychrophiles / low temperatureSlow reactions and reduced membrane fluidityCold-active enzymes and membrane-lipid adjustmentsSurvival while frozen does not demonstrate reproduction in ice.
Halophiles / high salinityOsmotic water loss and disrupted proteinsCompatible solutes or adapted “salt-in” strategiesSalt identity and water activity matter, not just salinity.
Acidophiles / low pHProton influx and macromolecular damageMembrane properties and pH-homeostasis mechanismsCytoplasm need not be as acidic as the environment.
Alkaliphiles / high pHMaintaining intracellular chemistry and energy couplingIon transport and pH regulationHigh external pH does not remove the need for gradients.
Piezophiles / high pressureChanges in proteins and membranesPressure-adapted enzymes and membrane compositionPressure interacts with temperature and chemistry.
Radiation-/desiccation-tolerant organismsDNA damage, oxidation, and water lossDNA repair and protection of cellular componentsTolerance does not imply use of radiation as an energy source.

Deinococcus radiodurans illustrates how efficient DNA repair, genome redundancy, and protection of repair machinery can contribute to radiation tolerance. Haloarchaea illustrate cellular solutions to osmotic stress. These are distinct solutions to distinct challenges: there is no generic “extreme-proof” organism that automatically tolerates every combination.

Worked astrobiology case: a hypothetical subsurface brine

Suppose an isolated candidate habitat is dark, cold, saline, and shielded by rock. A haloarchaeal analogue informs salt tolerance; a psychrophile informs cold adaptation; a chemolithotroph suggests a route to chemical energy. None alone establishes that one organism can grow under the combined conditions. Measure water activity, temperature, pH, compatible solutes or membrane traits, energy sources and sinks, nutrients, and persistence of those conditions.

Distinguish three conclusions: survival means remaining viable, growth means producing new biomass or cells, and habitability concerns whether an environment can support life under specified assumptions. A potentially habitable setting may be uninhabited. Detecting organics, methane, or DNA-like signals requires alternative explanations and contamination checks; terrestrial analogues guide investigations but do not prove life on Mars, Europa, or Enceladus.

Philippine learning contextUse a hypothetical Philippine geothermal spring or coastal saltern to practice matching measured conditions to adaptations. A site's appearance or location cannot establish that it contains a particular extremophile. Use published data or instructor-provided measurements and state which observations are missing.

Further reading: Temperature and Microbial Growth; NASA · Conditions Life Can Survive In.

§In class: integrate the evidence

  1. Guided cladogram construction. Use the supplied five-taxon, six-character matrix; identify nested shared states and explain the conflicting character.
  2. Taxonomic-key exercise. Classify four described samples, record every decision, and identify one limitation of the key.
  3. Extremophile case analysis. Distinguish measured growth from survival, match mechanisms to stresses, and state a testable habitability claim.

Submit the three components as Portfolio Entry 3: Phylogeny and extremophile analysis. Explain your decisions in your own words, label hypothetical data, cite external sources, and use peer feedback to check whether your conclusions follow from your evidence.

Before moving on, correct these misconceptions

  • Dominant does not mean common or advantageous.
  • Organisms do not acquire a needed mutation by intention.
  • A similar appearance does not automatically establish close ancestry.
  • A scientific name's second word alone is not a complete species name.
  • Archaea are not all extremophiles; microorganisms are not all bacteria.
  • An early branch is not a living ancestor, and a tree without a scale is not a clock.
  • Surviving stress does not demonstrate growth or an inhabited extraterrestrial environment.
